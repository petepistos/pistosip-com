Implementation Guide

Implementing Intune in a Google Workspace Office

A step-by-step guide for the person running the project. Email stays in Google. The computers come under proper management.

Audience: Owners, office managers, general ITEnvironment: Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 Business Premium for devicesDevices: Company-owned Windows, joinedVersion: 1.0

Your office runs on Google Workspace. Email, calendars and shared files live there and they are staying there. What this guide adds is proper management of the Windows computers themselves, using Microsoft Intune. The whole method is one idea repeated: decide first, test on one machine, then widen.

Why Intune

Google Workspace is a good home for email, calendars and files. Its tools for managing the Windows computers themselves are thin by comparison. Google can require encryption, push Windows updates and grant or remove local administrator rights, and it does those things through its own sign-in program on each machine, one user per device, with changes taking several hours to arrive. Intune is built to run the computer. The table shows the difference in plain terms.

CapabilityGoogle Workspace on its ownWith Intune added

EncryptionBitLocker can be required.BitLocker required, recovery keys stored in the tenant, and keys can be rotated remotely after anyone has seen one.

What stays where

Your email, calendar and files stay in Google. Nothing in this guide moves them. Intune manages the computer underneath. Each person gets a Microsoft account that is used to sign into Windows and nothing else. It is not a mailbox and nobody will receive email there.

Set expectations before you start

A twelve-person office is a six to ten week project. It is longer than the pure Microsoft version because each person's computer has to be moved onto a new sign-in, and that means moving their files and settings across. Most of the calendar time is waiting: for a pilot machine to behave normally for a few days, for people to be available, for a setting to prove it broke nothing.

Before you open the admin center

Four things decide whether this goes smoothly, and all four are decisions rather than settings.

Who owns each computer. A company-owned machine can be joined and fully managed. A personal machine usually cannot, and a Windows Home machine cannot be joined at all. Knowing which is which before you begin saves the most painful surprise in the project.

Two accounts per person, and you are fine with that. Everyone keeps their Google account for email and files. Everyone also gets a Microsoft account, with the same name, used only to sign into Windows. They look identical and they are not connected. That means two passwords and, when someone leaves, two places to switch them off. Write the leaver procedure down now.

What you are willing to break. Security settings restrict things. Decide in advance which trade-offs the business will accept, and get that in writing from whoever owns the business.

Who the emergency administrator is. Later in this guide you will turn on a rule that blocks unhealthy devices. If that rule catches every administrator at once, nobody can undo it. Set up a separate administrator account now and keep it out of every policy you write.

The twelve steps

1

Confirm the licenses and get into the Microsoft side

Why this matters

Intune comes with Microsoft 365 Business Premium. The office needs one Business Premium license per person whose computer will be managed, even though nobody will use it for email. Nothing further works until that is in place.

What you do
  • Confirm the business has a Microsoft 365 tenant and that someone can sign in to it as a global administrator. If nobody can, that is the first problem to solve, and it is a separate piece of work.
  • Confirm every person in scope holds a Business Premium license. Business Standard does not include Intune.
  • Sign in at intune.microsoft.com with an account holding the Intune Administrator role.
  • Go to Tenant administration, then Tenant status, and confirm the MDM authority reads Microsoft Intune.
How you know it worked

Tenant status shows Microsoft Intune as the MDM authority, and the license count matches the number of people in scope.

What goes wrong

The Microsoft tenant was set up years ago by someone who has since left, nobody has the password, and the project stalls for three weeks waiting on Microsoft support. Check access on day one, before anything else.

2

Prove your domain to Microsoft, and leave your mail alone

Why this matters

Right now Microsoft only knows the business as something.onmicrosoft.com. You want people signing into Windows as jane@yourcompany.com, the same name as their email. For that, Microsoft needs proof you own the domain.

What you do
  • In the Microsoft 365 admin center, go to Settings, then Domains, and add your domain.
  • Microsoft gives you one small text record to add at wherever your domain is managed (your registrar). That is the proof. Add it, wait a few minutes, come back and click verify.
  • When Microsoft offers to set up email and other services for the domain, decline them all. Mail stays with Google.
  • Confirm the mail records at the registrar still point to Google when you are done.
How you know it worked

The domain shows as verified in the Microsoft admin center, and email is still arriving normally in Google.

What goes wrong

Someone clicks through Microsoft's helpful setup wizard, which rewrites the mail records, and the office's email starts landing in empty Microsoft mailboxes. If email stops after this step, that is what happened. Put the records back.

3

Create a Microsoft account for each person, with the same name as their email

Why this matters

This is the account they will sign into Windows with. Giving it the same name as their Google address means nobody has to remember a second username, only a second password.

What you do
  • In the Microsoft 365 admin center, go to Users, then Active users, and add each person. Use their existing email address as the username.
  • Assign the Business Premium license to each one as you go.
  • Set a temporary password and have them change it at first sign-in.
  • Make clear to everyone, in writing, that this account is for signing into the computer only. No email will ever arrive there.
  • While you are there, create the emergency administrator account from the "before you start" section, and store its password somewhere safe and offline.
How you know it worked

Every person in scope appears in the user list with a license beside their name, plus one emergency administrator.

What goes wrong

Someone sets their Microsoft password to match their Google password because the names look the same. Two accounts with one password is worse than two passwords. Say so in the instructions.

4

Decide what you will manage, on paper, before touching a setting

Why this matters

Most Intune problems are planning problems wearing a technical costume. Ten minutes with a list prevents a week of untangling.

What you do

Build a simple list with four columns: person, computer, who owns it, and which bucket it falls into.

BucketWhat it coversWhat it gets
Fully managedCompany-owned Windows Pro or Enterprise computersJoined, enrolled, compliance rules, baseline, everything in this guide
Protected, not managedPersonal computers and phones used for work, including any Windows Home machineApp protection on company apps and multifactor sign-in. The device itself stays untouched
Left aloneA consultant's own laptop, or a machine running software that cannot tolerate changeNothing, by deliberate decision, recorded with the reason and who approved it
How you know it worked

For every computer in the office, you can say out loud which bucket it is in and why.

What goes wrong

Finding out in week three that the person who runs the office is on a personal Windows Home machine. Windows Home cannot be joined. Make that discovery now.

5

Create your groups before you create any policy

Why this matters

Every rule in Intune is pointed at a group. If the groups do not exist yet you will point rules at "All devices" because it is the only option on the screen, and that is how people lock themselves out.

What you do
  • Create a device group for the machines that will be managed.
  • Create a second device group for machines that must be excluded, even if it has one member today.
  • Create a small pilot group holding your first test machine.
  • When you need to keep one machine out of a policy, use the exclusion group rather than an assignment filter. Exclusions are visible on the policy's assignment page. Filters are easy to forget.
How you know it worked

Three groups exist and each contains exactly the devices you expect. No policies exist yet.

What goes wrong

A one-off exclusion set up as a filter, forgotten six months later, and nobody can explain why one machine is unmanaged.

6

Turn on automatic enrollment, scoped to the pilot group

Why this matters

Automatic enrollment means that when a person signs into Windows with their new Microsoft account, the computer joins the business and enrolls itself. The alternative is doing it by hand at every desk.

What you do
  • In the Intune admin center, go to Devices, then Enrollment, then the Windows tab, then Automatic Enrollment.
  • Set MDM user scope to Some and choose your pilot group. Do not set it to All yet.
  • Leave MAM user scope at None.
  • Leave the three URLs at their defaults.
How you know it worked

A test user in the pilot group can sign into a machine with their Microsoft account, and that machine appears in Intune within a few minutes.

What goes wrong

Setting the scope to All on day one. Every machine anyone signs into starts enrolling itself, including personal laptops nobody meant to manage.

7

Join one machine and prove it works

Why this matters

The pilot is where the surprises live, and it is the cheapest place to find them. This step is also where you learn what moving a person onto a new sign-in actually involves, before you do it twenty times.

What you do
  • Pick one company-owned Windows computer and one patient person.
  • Before you touch it, save their files, browser bookmarks and anything on the desktop somewhere safe. Joining the machine gives them a fresh Windows profile. The old one is still there, but nothing moves across on its own.
  • On the machine, open Settings, then Accounts, then Access work or school, then Connect. Choose Join this device to Microsoft Entra ID and sign in with the new Microsoft account. Restart.
  • Sign in as the Microsoft account. Move their files and settings into the new profile. Sign into Chrome and Google Drive with their Google account as before.
  • In the Intune admin center, confirm the device shows as Microsoft Entra joined and ownership reads Corporate.
  • Leave it alone for two or three days of ordinary work before you apply anything to it.
How you know it worked

The device is listed as Entra joined with a recent check-in, the person has their files, email and Drive working, and they have not had to think about Microsoft since the first restart.

What goes wrong

The machine shows as registered rather than joined. Registered means the account was added on top of the old sign-in and the computer is not managed. The policies in later steps will never reach it. Start the join again and make sure you pick the join option, not the "add account" option.

8

Write the compliance rules

Why this matters

A compliance policy is the list of conditions a computer must meet to count as healthy. On its own it only reports. It becomes enforcement in Step 10.

What you do

Start short. Five rules most small offices can meet today:

  • Disk encryption turned on (BitLocker)
  • Antivirus on and up to date. Defender for Business, which comes with the license, satisfies this
  • Firewall on
  • A minimum Windows version, set at something your fleet already runs
  • A password or PIN required to unlock the machine

Set the grace period to a few days so people have time to fix a problem before anything stops working.

How you know it worked

The pilot machine reports Compliant, or tells you precisely which rule it fails and why.

What goes wrong

Switching on a dozen rules at once and spending a week explaining amber warnings to staff who did nothing wrong.

9

Apply the security baseline, to the pilot machine only

Why this matters

A security baseline is Microsoft's prepackaged set of several hundred hardening settings, kept current as Windows changes. It saves you from inventing a standard from scratch and then maintaining it forever.

What you do
  • Go to Endpoint security, then Security baselines, and create a profile from the Windows security baseline.
  • Read the settings rather than accepting them blindly. Password rules and legacy protocol settings are the two areas most often adjusted for a small office.
  • Assign the profile to your pilot group, and add the exclusion group as an exclusion while you are on that screen.
  • Give it several days of normal work before going any further.
  • Keep the stricter settings you may want later, such as blocking USB drives or removing local admin rights, in a separate, clearly named profile with its own schedule.
Worth knowing

Microsoft's baselines are not certified against CIS or NIST. They mirror those recommendations closely and they are a reasonable starting point, though they do not by themselves answer a regulator or a carrier asking for a formal control mapping.

How you know it worked

The baseline reports as applied with no errors, and after several days nobody's daily work has broken.

What goes wrong

Assigning the baseline to every machine the day you create it. When something breaks you are left guessing which of several hundred settings caused it.

10

Turn on Conditional Access, in report-only mode first

Why this matters

Until this step the compliance rules only report. Conditional Access gives them teeth. In a Google office it protects less than it would in a full Microsoft office, since email is in Google, but it still forces every machine to stay healthy, and it protects any Microsoft data the business does use.

What you do
  • Confirm the emergency administrator account from Step 3 exists, and exclude it from every policy you write here. Do this before anything else on this list.
  • In the Entra admin center, create a Conditional Access policy requiring a compliant device, in report-only mode.
  • Watch the report for a week. Fix the machines that would have failed.
  • Only then switch the policy on.
How you know it worked

A healthy machine gets through, a deliberately noncompliant test machine does not, and you can still sign in with the emergency account.

What goes wrong

Turning it on with no exclusion and locking every administrator out of the tenant at once, including the account needed to undo it.

11

Join the rest, two or three at a time

Why this matters

Every join is a small disruption for the person: a new sign-in, a fresh profile, half an hour of moving things across. Spread that out so the office never has more than two people out of action at once.

What you do
  • Widen the MDM user scope from the pilot group to the full managed group.
  • Book each person a half-hour slot. Follow exactly the Step 7 routine: back up, join, restart, sign in, move files, reconnect Chrome and Drive.
  • Two or three machines per day, no more. Check each one shows as Entra joined and compliant before you move on.
  • Keep a running list of who is done and who is next, and share it with the office so people know when their turn is.
How you know it worked

The device count in Intune matches your inventory list from Step 4, every machine is joined rather than registered, and the compliance figure sits at or near one hundred percent.

What goes wrong

Doing twelve on a Friday afternoon. One problem becomes twelve problems, and nobody can send email on Monday.

12

Keep it running, and keep the two account systems in step

Why this matters

Intune is not a project that finishes. And in a Google office there is a second standing job: two sets of accounts that nothing keeps synchronised.

What you do
  • Set a monthly habit: review noncompliant devices, review failed policy assignments, read what Microsoft changed in the baseline, and confirm any new hire's machine actually joined.
  • Write a leaver procedure with two lines in it: disable the Google account, disable the Microsoft account. Miss the second and they still have a live sign-in to a company computer.
  • Write a starter procedure with two lines in it as well, so new people get both accounts on day one.
  • Keep a written record of what you configured and when. If a regulator, a carrier or an auditor asks how you manage endpoints, that record is the answer.
Worth knowing

If managing two passwords per person becomes a burden, Microsoft can be made the sign-in for Google as well, so people have one password and one second factor, and switching someone off in Microsoft switches them off in Google automatically. That is a separate, short project and it is included in the license. Keep one Google administrator outside that arrangement so you can always get back in.

How you know it worked

Six months on, the device count still matches the inventory, the compliance figure is still green, and the last person who left is disabled in both places.

What goes wrong

Treating go-live as the finish line. Six months later half the fleet is noncompliant and a leaver still signs in, because after go-live nobody was looking.

The six mistakes that cause the most damage

  1. Assigning a policy to All devices. It is the default-looking option and it reaches machines you never intended, including the one you are working from.
  2. Skipping the pilot. Every hour saved here costs a day later, and it costs it in front of the whole office.
  3. No break-glass account excluded from Conditional Access. The one mistake that can lock you out of fixing the other five.
  4. Registering machines instead of joining them. They appear in the console and look managed, and nothing you deploy ever lands.
  5. One giant policy profile. It cannot be partly undone, so the first real problem turns into switching everything off.
  6. Using assignment filters for one-off exceptions. They work, they are invisible on the assignment page, and nobody remembers them six months on. Use an exclusion group.

Plain-English glossary

Tenant
Your company's own space inside Microsoft's cloud. Every account, device and policy lives in it.
Microsoft Entra ID
The directory of who works here. Older documentation calls it Azure Active Directory.
Intune
Microsoft's tool for managing computers and phones remotely. Included in Business Premium.
Joined
The computer belongs to the business and signs into Windows with the Microsoft account. Intune runs it.
Registered
A work account was added to a computer the business does not run. Policies do not reach it. Not what you want for company machines.
Enrollment
The moment a joined computer starts checking in with Intune for instructions.
Compliance policy
The health check. A list of conditions a computer must meet, such as encryption on and antivirus running.
Configuration profile
The settings you push out. Anything you want switched on, off or set to a particular value.
Security baseline
A prepackaged configuration profile from Microsoft with several hundred recommended hardening settings.
Conditional Access
The gate. The rule that decides whether a sign-in is allowed, based on who, from where, and on what kind of device.
MDM user scope
The switch that controls who gets automatic enrollment. Set to None, Some, or All.
Autopilot
A way of shipping a new computer straight from the supplier to the employee, configured on first sign-in without IT touching it.
Break-glass account
A spare administrator account kept out of every policy, with its password stored offline, so you can always get back in.
Google Workspace
Where your email, calendar and files live. Nothing in this guide changes that.
Profile
A person's desktop, documents, settings and browser inside Windows. A new sign-in gets a new, empty one.

Project checklist

  • Microsoft tenant reachable by a global administrator, Business Premium license for every person in scope
  • Domain verified in Microsoft with one text record, mail records still pointing to Google
  • Microsoft account created for each person, same name as their email, license assigned
  • Emergency administrator account created, password stored offline
  • Every computer assigned to one of the three buckets, in writing
  • Managed, exclusion and pilot groups created, with no policies yet
  • Automatic enrollment turned on, scoped to the pilot group only
  • One machine joined (not registered), person's files moved across, left alone for several days
  • Compliance rules written, five to start, with a grace period set
  • Security baseline applied to the pilot machine, stricter settings held in a separate profile
  • Conditional Access run in report-only for a full week before being switched on
  • Remaining machines joined in batches of two or three
  • Starter and leaver procedures written, covering both the Google and Microsoft accounts
  • Monthly review scheduled, with an owner's name against it
