Implementation Guide

Implementing Intune in a Microsoft 365 Office

A step-by-step guide for the person running the project. Sign-in, email and devices already live in one place, which makes this the simpler version.

Audience: Owners, office managers, general ITEnvironment: Microsoft 365 Business PremiumDevices: Company-owned WindowsVersion: 1.0

Your office already runs on Microsoft 365: email in Outlook, files in OneDrive, meetings in Teams. What this guide adds is proper management of the computers themselves, using Intune, which is included in Microsoft 365 Business Premium. Because sign-in, email and devices all live in the same place, this is the simpler version of the project. The whole method is one idea repeated: decide first, test on one machine, then widen.

Why Intune

Every Microsoft 365 business plan includes a small device management feature called Basic Mobility and Security. It can wipe a lost phone and set a few rules on it, and that is roughly where it stops. Intune, which comes with Microsoft 365 Business Premium, is the full version. If the business is on Business Standard today, it has the first column. Moving to Business Premium gives it the second, together with Defender for Business and the sign-in controls that make the second column enforceable.

The license line

Intune is included in Microsoft 365 Business Premium. It is not in Business Basic or Business Standard, and buying Intune on its own costs more than the difference between the plans. If the business is on Standard, the first step is to move the licenses to Premium, which also brings Defender for Business and the Conditional Access feature this guide relies on.

Set expectations before you start

A twelve-person office is a four to eight week project, not a weekend. Most of that time is waiting: for a pilot machine to behave normally for a few days, for people to be available, for a setting to prove it broke nothing. Rushing is what turns an Intune rollout into an outage.

Before you open the admin center

Three things decide whether this goes smoothly, and all three are decisions rather than settings.

Who owns each computer. A company-owned machine can be fully managed. A personal machine usually cannot, and a machine running Windows Home edition cannot be enrolled at all. Knowing which is which before you begin saves the most painful surprise in the project.

What you are willing to break. Security settings restrict things. Some restrictions are invisible to staff and some stop them working. Decide in advance which trade-offs the business will accept, and get that in writing from whoever owns the business.

Who the emergency administrator is. Later in this guide you will turn on a rule that blocks unhealthy devices. If that rule catches every administrator at once, nobody can undo it. Set up a separate administrator account now and keep it out of every policy you write.

The ten steps

1

Confirm the licensing and make Intune the manager of record

Why this matters

Business Premium includes everything this project needs. The trap is the plan sitting next to it on the price list: Business Standard looks almost identical on an invoice and contains no device management at all.

What you do
  • Confirm every person whose computer you will manage holds a Microsoft 365 Business Premium license. If the office is on Standard, move the licenses first. Nothing else in this guide works until that is done.
  • Sign in at intune.microsoft.com with an account holding the Intune Administrator role.
  • Go to Tenant administration, then Tenant status. Confirm the MDM authority reads Microsoft Intune, and that the license count matches the number of people in scope.
  • Create the emergency administrator account now, and store its password somewhere safe and offline.
What the plan does and does not cover
Included in Business PremiumNot included, and chargeable if anyone suggests it

Microsoft Entra ID P1. Conditional Access, multifactor authentication, dynamic groups and Windows Autopilot. That is Steps 3, 4 and 9.Remote Help. Microsoft's own screen-sharing tool. Most small offices do not need it; see the note after Step 10.
Defender for Business. The antivirus and endpoint protection that the compliance rules in Step 6 check for.Remediations (scheduled detect-and-fix scripts), which need Windows Enterprise licensing, plus the rest of the Intune Suite.

Nothing in this guide needs anything from the right-hand column.

How you know it worked

Tenant status shows Microsoft Intune as the MDM authority and the license count matches your headcount.

What goes wrong

An office was sold Business Standard. Email, Teams and Office all work, so nothing looks wrong, until enrollment fails on every machine with an error that never once mentions licensing.

2

Decide what you will manage, on paper, before touching a setting

Why this matters

Most Intune problems are planning problems wearing a technical costume. Ten minutes with a list prevents a week of untangling.

What you do

Build a simple list with four columns: person, computer, who owns it, and which bucket it falls into.

BucketWhat it coversWhat it gets
Fully managedCompany-owned Windows Pro or Enterprise computersJoined, enrolled, compliance rules, baseline, everything in this guide
Protected, not managedPersonal computers and phones used for work, including any Windows Home machineApp protection on company apps and multifactor sign-in. The device itself stays untouched
Left aloneA consultant's own laptop, or a machine running software that cannot tolerate changeNothing, by deliberate decision, recorded with the reason and who approved it
How you know it worked

For every computer in the office, you can say out loud which bucket it is in and why.

What goes wrong

Finding out mid-rollout that the person who runs the office is on a personal Windows Home machine. Make that discovery now.

3

Create your groups before you create any policy

Why this matters

Every rule in Intune is pointed at a group. If the groups do not exist yet, you will point rules at "All devices" because it is the only option on the screen, and that is how people lock themselves out.

What you do
  • Create a device group for the machines that will be managed.
  • Create a second device group for machines that must be excluded, even if it has one member today.
  • Create a small pilot group holding your first test machine.
  • When you need to keep one machine out of a policy, use the exclusion group rather than an assignment filter.
How you know it worked

Three groups exist and each contains exactly the devices you expect. No policies exist yet.

What goes wrong

A one-off exclusion set up as a filter, forgotten six months later, and nobody can explain why one machine is unmanaged.

4

Turn on automatic enrollment, scoped to the pilot group

Why this matters

Automatic enrollment means a person signs into Windows with their work account and the computer joins the business and enrolls itself. The alternative is doing it by hand at every desk.

What you do
  • In the Intune admin center, go to Devices, then Enrollment, then the Windows tab, then Automatic Enrollment.
  • Set MDM user scope to Some and choose your pilot group. Do not set it to All yet.
  • Leave MAM user scope at None unless you are also protecting personal phones, which is a separate exercise.
  • Leave the three URLs at their defaults.
How you know it worked

A test user in the pilot group can sign into a machine with their work account, and that machine appears in Intune within a few minutes.

What goes wrong

Setting the scope to All on day one. Every machine anyone signs into starts enrolling itself, including personal laptops nobody meant to manage.

5

Enroll one machine and prove it works

Why this matters

The pilot is where the surprises live, and it is the cheapest place to find them. One machine and one patient person is the whole requirement.

What you do
  • On a company-owned Windows computer that is already signed in with the work account, enrollment may already have happened the moment you changed Step 4. Check the Intune device list first.
  • If not, open Settings, then Accounts, then Access work or school. If the work account is listed and says the device is connected to the organization, it is joined. If it is missing, connect it, choosing Join this device to Microsoft Entra ID.
  • On a machine being set up from new, signing in with the work account during first-time setup joins and enrolls it in one pass.
  • In the Intune admin center, confirm the device shows as Microsoft Entra joined and ownership reads Corporate.
  • Leave it alone for two or three days of ordinary work before you apply anything to it.
How you know it worked

The device is listed, shows a recent check-in time, reports as Entra joined, and the person using it has not noticed any difference.

What goes wrong

The machine shows as registered rather than joined. Registered means the account is connected but the computer is not managed, and the policies in the following steps will never reach it. That usually means a personal or local account was signed in first.

6

Write the compliance rules

Why this matters

A compliance policy is the list of conditions a computer must meet to count as healthy. On its own it only reports. It becomes enforcement in Step 9.

What you do

Start short. Five rules most small offices can meet today:

  • Disk encryption turned on (BitLocker)
  • Antivirus on and up to date. Defender for Business, which comes with your plan, satisfies this
  • Firewall on
  • A minimum Windows version, set at something your fleet already runs
  • A password or PIN required to unlock the machine

Set the grace period to a few days. That gives people time to fix a problem before anything they rely on stops working.

How you know it worked

The pilot machine reports Compliant, or tells you precisely which rule it fails and why. Either outcome is a success at this stage.

What goes wrong

Switching on a dozen rules at once, then spending a week explaining amber warnings to staff who did nothing wrong.

7

Apply the security baseline, to the pilot machine only

Why this matters

A security baseline is Microsoft's prepackaged set of several hundred hardening settings, kept current as Windows changes. It saves you from inventing a standard from scratch and then maintaining it forever.

What you do
  • Go to Endpoint security, then Security baselines, and create a profile from the Windows security baseline.
  • Read the settings rather than accepting them blindly. Password rules and legacy protocol settings are the two areas most often adjusted for a small office.
  • Assign the profile to your pilot group, and add the exclusion group as an exclusion while you are on that screen.
  • Give it several days of normal work before going any further.
Worth knowing

Microsoft's baselines are not certified against CIS or NIST. They mirror those recommendations closely, and they are a reasonable starting point, though they do not by themselves answer a regulator or a carrier asking for a formal control mapping.

How you know it worked

The baseline reports as applied with no errors, and after several days nobody's daily work has broken.

What goes wrong

Assigning the baseline to every machine the day you create it. When something breaks you are left guessing which of several hundred settings caused it.

8

Keep the stricter settings in a separate profile

Why this matters

The baseline is the set of settings almost every business should have. Past it sit the intrusive ones: blocking USB storage, removing local administrator rights, controlling which applications can run. Those change how people work, and they need their own schedule.

What you do
  • Keep the baseline as one profile and the tightening as a second, clearly named profile.
  • Roll the second one out later, on its own timeline, one group of settings at a time.
  • Write down every setting the business asked you to leave off, who asked, and when.
  • While you are here, turn on Windows LAPS under Endpoint security, then Account protection. It gives every machine its own local administrator password, rotated automatically, and replaces whatever shared password the office has been using.
How you know it worked

You can point at one profile and say "this is the standard," at a second and say "this is the tightening," and at a short list and say "this is what we deferred, and here is who decided."

What goes wrong

Blending everything into one profile. When the aggressive part breaks something there is no clean way to back it out without losing the baseline too.

9

Turn on Conditional Access, in report-only mode first

Why this matters

Until this step, compliance rules only report. Conditional Access is the rule that gives them teeth: email, files and Teams are reachable only from a device Intune considers healthy. In a Microsoft office this protects everything, which is why it is worth the care. It is also the single most dangerous switch in this guide.

What you do
  • Confirm the emergency administrator account from Step 1 exists, and exclude it from every Conditional Access policy you write. Do this before anything else on this list.
  • In the Entra admin center, create a policy requiring a compliant device, in report-only mode, which records who would have been blocked without blocking anyone.
  • Watch the report for a week. Fix the machines that would have failed.
  • Only then switch the policy on.
How you know it worked

A healthy machine gets through, a deliberately noncompliant test machine does not, and you can still sign in with the emergency account from a device the policy would otherwise block.

What goes wrong

Turning on Conditional Access with no exclusion and locking every administrator out of the tenant at once, including the account needed to undo it.

10

Enroll the rest, then keep it running

Why this matters

Intune is not a project that finishes. Windows changes, Microsoft updates the baseline, staff get new machines, and a fleet left unwatched drifts out of compliance quietly.

What you do
  • Widen the MDM user scope from the pilot group to the full managed group.
  • Enroll the remaining machines in small batches, two or three at a time rather than all at once.
  • For new computers, use Autopilot: register the machine before it ships and it configures itself the first time the employee signs in.
  • Set a monthly habit: review noncompliant devices, review failed policy assignments, read what Microsoft changed in the baseline, and confirm any new hire's machine actually enrolled.
  • Keep a written record of what you configured and when. If a regulator, a carrier or an auditor asks how you manage endpoints, that record is the answer.
How you know it worked

The device count in Intune matches your inventory list from Step 2, and the compliance figure sits at or near one hundred percent and stays there.

What goes wrong

Treating go-live as the finish line. Six months later half the fleet is noncompliant and nobody noticed, because after go-live nobody was looking.

Remote management and remote support

Two different things get called remote management, and conflating them leads to buying something you did not need.

  • Managing a machine you cannot physically reach. Pushing settings, installing software, restarting it, wiping it. Business Premium already does all of this, from the device page in the Intune admin center.
  • Watching and driving someone's screen while they sit at it. This is not in Business Premium. If the person who supports these computers is in the building and holds the local administrator password, you do not need it. If support comes from outside the building, or someone needs to work on a machine nobody is sitting at, you need a tool for it, and it needs to keep a record of who connected to what.

For a small office supporting itself, the free Windows Quick Assist is workable for occasional help at the desk. It keeps no records and cannot elevate to administrator, so it does not survive an examination as the standing method. An outside provider will usually bring their own tool, priced per technician, and that tool should be in your written access agreement with them.

The six mistakes that cause the most damage

  1. Assigning a policy to All devices. It is the default-looking option and it reaches machines you never intended, including the one you are working from.
  2. Skipping the pilot. Every hour saved here costs a day later, and it costs it in front of the whole office.
  3. No break-glass account excluded from Conditional Access. The one mistake that can lock you out of fixing the other five.
  4. Registering machines instead of joining them. They appear in the console and look managed, and nothing you deploy ever lands.
  5. One giant policy profile. It cannot be partly undone, so the first real problem turns into switching everything off.
  6. Using assignment filters for one-off exceptions. They work, they are invisible on the assignment page, and nobody remembers them six months on. Use an exclusion group.

Plain-English glossary

Tenant
Your company's own space inside Microsoft's cloud. Every account, device and policy lives in it.
Microsoft Entra ID
The directory of who works here. Older documentation calls it Azure Active Directory.
Intune
Microsoft's tool for managing computers and phones remotely. Included in Business Premium.
Joined
The computer belongs to the business and signs into Windows with the Microsoft account. Intune runs it.
Registered
A work account was added to a computer the business does not run. Policies do not reach it. Not what you want for company machines.
Enrollment
The moment a joined computer starts checking in with Intune for instructions.
Compliance policy
The health check. A list of conditions a computer must meet, such as encryption on and antivirus running.
Configuration profile
The settings you push out. Anything you want switched on, off or set to a particular value.
Security baseline
A prepackaged configuration profile from Microsoft with several hundred recommended hardening settings.
Conditional Access
The gate. The rule that decides whether a sign-in is allowed, based on who, from where, and on what kind of device.
MDM user scope
The switch that controls who gets automatic enrollment. Set to None, Some, or All.
Autopilot
A way of shipping a new computer straight from the supplier to the employee, configured on first sign-in without IT touching it.
Break-glass account
A spare administrator account kept out of every policy, with its password stored offline, so you can always get back in.
Windows LAPS
A feature that gives every machine its own local administrator password, rotates it, and stores it where only the right people can read it.
Defender for Business
The antivirus and endpoint protection included in Business Premium, managed from the same console as Intune.

Project checklist

  • Business Premium confirmed for every person in scope, and Intune is the MDM authority
  • Emergency administrator account created, password stored offline
  • Every computer in the office assigned to one of the three buckets, in writing
  • Managed, exclusion and pilot groups created, with no policies yet
  • Automatic enrollment turned on, scoped to the pilot group only
  • One machine enrolled, showing as Entra joined and corporate-owned
  • Compliance rules written, five to start, with a grace period set
  • Security baseline applied to the pilot machine and left alone for several days
  • Stricter settings held in a separate, named profile for later; Windows LAPS turned on
  • Conditional Access run in report-only mode for a full week before being switched on
  • Remaining machines enrolled in small batches; Autopilot used for new hardware
  • Monthly review scheduled, with an owner's name against it
  • Written record of the configuration kept where an auditor could be shown it
